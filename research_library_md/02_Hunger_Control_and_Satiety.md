# 02 — Hunger Control and Satiety

*Phase 2 module, file 2 of 12. Depends on `01_High_Volume_Low_Calorie_Foods.md` (which foods) — this file explains **why** and turns it into a daily protocol.*

---

## 1. Purpose

Fat loss fails mostly for one reason: **hunger wins**. This file separates the hunger-control levers into those with real human evidence, those with only mechanistic plausibility, and those that are folklore — then assembles them into one protocol you can run with food available in Mogadishu.

**Out of scope:** calorie targets (deliberately not set here — see Phase-1 `02_FAST_FAT_LOSS.md`), obesity medication (Phase-1 `17_EXPENSIVE_DRUGS_AND_AFFORDABLE_ALTERNATIVES.md`), fasting schedules (file `03`).

---

## 2. The one-paragraph model

Hunger is not a single signal. It is the sum of (a) **mechanical** signals from the gut (how full, how fast it empties), (b) **hormonal** signals (ghrelin up before meals; GLP-1, PYY, CCK up after), (c) **nutrient-status** signals (protein and energy deficit increase drive), (d) **hedonic** signals (palatability, cue-triggered wanting), and (e) **state** factors (sleep, stress, alcohol, boredom). Volume eating works through (a) and (d); protein works through (b) and (c); sleep works through (c) and (d). **No single lever is powerful; stacking 4–5 is.**

## 3. Mechanism-by-mechanism assessment

For each: what it does → evidence → practical action → clinical meaningfulness → cost.

### 3.1 Stomach distension (gastric mechanoreception)

- **What it does:** stretch receptors in the stomach wall signal fullness to the brainstem. Larger gastric volume → earlier satiety.
- **Evidence:** Direct human evidence comes from bariatric surgery. After sleeve gastrectomy (which removes most of the stomach), patients report satiety at much smaller food volumes, and gastric distension is the proposed mechanism for reduced intake ([Melissas 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17355769/)). This is *indirect* for a person with an intact stomach, but it is the best human evidence that gastric volume is causally linked to fullness.
- **Practical action:** eat foods with high water and fibre content (file `01`); drink water with meals if that helps you; eat soup first ([Flood & Rolls 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574705/)).
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes — this is the largest single contributor to fullness from food choice, and it is free.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.2 Energy density (kcal per gram of food)

- **What it does:** people tend to eat a roughly constant **weight/volume** of food day to day. Lower energy density therefore means fewer calories at the same fullness.
- **Evidence:** Reducing dietary energy density lowers ad libitum intake in controlled studies; in a randomised trial, a low-energy-density diet improved weight-loss maintenance and cardiovascular risk markers ([Karimi 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27904578/)). The strongest single demonstration is the controlled feeding study in which identical-looking ultra-processed vs unprocessed diets differed by **+508 kcal/day** on the ultra-processed diet ([Hall 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/)) — largely an energy-density and eating-rate effect.
- **Practical action:** never eat "dry" calories as the bulk of a meal; add water-rich vegetables and soup; cook with measured oil, not poured oil.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes, high. This is the mechanism behind "volume eating."
- **Cost:** Free/saves money (vegetables are cheaper than meat and oil).

### 3.3 Protein

- **What it does:** protein is the most satiating macronutrient per calorie; it also raises thermogenesis modestly, and higher-protein diets preserve lean mass in a deficit.
- **Evidence:** High-protein vs normal-protein isocaloric meals increased satiety and suppressed ghrelin in a randomised crossover ([Yang 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23824257/)). A critical review of high-protein diets found consistent satiety and thermogenesis advantages ([Halton 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15466943/)). In a 12-week inpatient-style study, raising protein to ~30% of energy produced a spontaneous **−441 kcal/day** intake and weight loss without deliberate restriction ([Weigle 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/)). Population data are consistent with "protein leverage" — appetite driven until a protein target is met ([Eriksen 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42102975/)), though this remains an observational/mechanistic framework, not a proven clinical rule.
- **Practical action:** 25–40 g protein per main meal; protein first or at least not last; see §5 for distribution.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes. Effect size is moderate but reliable, and it doubles as muscle protection (`06`).
- **Cost:** Cheap in this market (eggs, chicken, goat, fish, lentils).

### 3.4 Fibre and gastric emptying

- **What it does:** viscous soluble fibres (psyllium, oat β-glucan, okra, legumes) form gels that slow gastric emptying and blunt post-meal glucose; insoluble fibre adds bulk and shortens transit.
- **Evidence:** Pulses improve appetite control in overweight/obese adults ([John 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37395146/)). A high-protein, high-fibre **beverage preload** reduced subsequent ad libitum intake ([Sharafi 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29955731/)). Fibre intake 25–29 g/day is the range associated with best outcomes ([Reynolds 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/)). Psyllium specifically improves stool form ([Suares 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21332763/)) and LDL cholesterol ([Jovanovski 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30239559/)); its *weight-loss* effect is small (see `07` §6).
- **Practical action:** 25–35 g/day from food; lentils/beans/okra/oats/cabbage; increase slowly.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes for appetite and bowel function; **small** for scale weight on its own.
- **Cost:** Very cheap from food; psyllium is the only supplement worth considering (`09`).

### 3.5 Liquid vs solid food form

- **What it does:** liquids empty faster, require no chewing, and produce weaker satiety per calorie.
- **Evidence:** Beverage form produced weaker compensation than solid food of equal energy ([Cassady 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22258267/)). Water *as a preload* before a meal does reduce intake, however — the exception that proves the rule, because water has zero calories and adds volume ([Daniels 2010 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20796216/); Phase-1 also recorded a ~2 kg extra loss trial with 500 ml pre-meal water, [Dennis 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19661958/)).
- **Practical action:** eat fruit, not juice; eat soup with a spoon, not drink smoothies; 400–500 ml water 15–30 min before the main meal.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Moderate. The juice/smoothie trap is a real source of several hundred unaccounted calories a day.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.6 Ghrelin (the "hunger hormone")

- **What it does:** rises before habitual meals, falls after eating; suppressed more by protein and by calories than by volume alone.
- **Evidence:** Protein meals suppress ghrelin more than isoenergetic lower-protein meals ([Yang 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23824257/)). After diet-induced weight loss, ghrelin rises and stays elevated at 1 year alongside reduced leptin and increased hunger — this is the physiology of regain ([Sumithran 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22029981/)).
- **Practical action:** you cannot "block" ghrelin with food, supplements or tricks. You can (i) keep protein high, (ii) keep meals on a predictable schedule so the pre-meal rise is expected, (iii) accept that some hunger in a deficit is normal and not a sign of failure.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes, but as an explanation rather than a lever.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.7 GLP-1, PYY, CCK (post-meal satiety hormones)

- **What they do:** released from the gut in response to nutrients; slow gastric emptying, signal satiety.
- **Evidence:** In a randomised crossover, fat, protein and carbohydrate loads produced different CCK and PYY responses and different appetite ratings ([Brennan 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22556143/)); CCK is an established satiety mediator ([Desai 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28685026/)). Soluble fibre and protein are the food components that raise them most.
- **Critical caveat:** the post-meal physiological rise is **far smaller and shorter** than pharmacological GLP-1 receptor agonism. **No food reproduces a GLP-1 drug effect.** Foods modulate appetite; drugs produce 15–21% body-weight loss. These are not comparable.
- **Practical action:** fibre + protein at every meal is the only food-level lever.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Modest.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.8 Leptin

- **What it does:** fat-derived hormone that suppresses appetite; falls sharply with fat loss — more than the fat loss itself would predict.
- **Evidence:** After ~10% weight loss, leptin fell and hunger increased, persisting at 1 year ([Sumithran 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22029981/)).
- **Practical action:** none available. Leptin resistance in obesity and the fall after loss are not fixable by diet, food timing, or supplements. Sleep deprivation lowers leptin and raises ghrelin (see 3.10).
- **Clinically meaningful?** As an explanation for why maintenance is harder than loss. Not a lever.
- **Cost:** N/A.

### 3.9 Insulin

- **What it does:** suppresses appetite in the short term in some models; the popular claim that "insulin blocks fat burning" is a misreading of lipolysis regulation.
- **Evidence:** **Weak for the popular claim.** The "carbohydrate-insulin model" of obesity has failed to produce consistent human support: in controlled isocaloric comparisons, low-carbohydrate and low-fat diets produce similar fat loss. Insulin does not need to be "controlled" for fat loss to occur; energy balance does.
- **Practical action:** choose carbohydrates by **satiety and fibre**, not by insulin fear. The low-GI approach is reasonable for appetite and glucose control but is not required for fat loss.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Only if you have diabetes/prediabetes, where carbohydrate distribution matters medically (Phase-1 and `03` §8).
- **Cost:** N/A.

### 3.10 Sleep deprivation

- **What it does:** raises ghrelin, lowers leptin, increases hedonic drive to energy-dense food, and reduces insulin sensitivity.
- **Evidence:** Under 5.5 h vs 8.5 h sleep in a controlled inpatient study with matched calories, participants lost **0.6 kg fat vs 1.4 kg** and lost **60% more fat-free mass** ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)). Extending sleep to 8.5 h in short sleepers reduced spontaneous intake by **~270 kcal/day** ([Tasali 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35129580/)).
- **Practical action:** see `05` — this is the highest-yield non-food intervention in the module.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes, high.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.11 Stress and emotional eating

- **What it does:** cortisol and negative affect increase intake of energy-dense, highly palatable food in susceptible people; "stress eating" is real but highly individual.
- **Evidence:** **Mixed and mostly observational.** The link between chronic stress and weight gain is inconsistent; the most useful practical framing is behavioural (cue removal, alternative responses, planned meals) rather than hormonal. Deliberately, no effect-size claims are made here because the literature does not support a confident one.
- **Practical action:** (i) do not stock trigger foods; (ii) pre-portion snacks; (iii) use a 10-minute delay rule; (iv) replace the *function* of the behaviour (e.g. walk, shower, call someone) rather than fighting the urge.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Variable. If eating is genuinely out of control or accompanied by purging/guilt cycles, see `04` §6 — that is a clinical issue, not a diet-planning issue.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.12 Palatability and hyper-palatability

- **What it does:** combinations of fat + refined carbohydrate + salt (the "hyper-palatable" definition) increase eating rate and delay satiety signals.
- **Evidence:** A quantitative definition of hyper-palatable foods was validated and applied to the US food supply ([Fazzino 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31689013/)); the Hall 2019 ward study showed such diets drive +508 kcal/day ([Hall 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/)).
- **Practical action:** keep meals "tasty but plain" — one flavour dimension, not fat+sweet+salt simultaneously; do not eat from a shared package; do not combine dates/honey with nuts and oil "as a healthy snack."
- **Clinically meaningful?** Yes — this is the mechanism by which "healthy" foods become fattening.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.13 Eating speed and chewing

- **What it does:** faster eating → more calories before satiety signals arrive.
- **Evidence:** Eating rate has **sustained effects on ad libitum energy intake** over 2 weeks in a randomised dietary trial ([Forde 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41314613/)); texture/food form manipulations that slow eating reduce intake ([van Bruinessen 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41492689/)).
- **Practical action:** put the fork down between bites; use a spoon instead of fingers for rice; choose foods needing chewing (raw carrot, cabbage, whole fruit, meat on the bone).
- **Clinically meaningful?** Moderate, and free.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.14 Sensory-specific satiety and meal structure

- **What it does:** satiety is partly learned/associative; foods with satiety-congruent sensory qualities are more filling, and variety within a meal increases intake.
- **Evidence:** Satiety-relevant sensory qualities enhanced the satiating effect of mixed carbohydrate-protein preloads ([Yeomans 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22030223/)).
- **Practical action:** fewer distinct flavours per meal, more volume per dish; keep the same breakfast most days.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Small–moderate.
- **Cost:** Free.

### 3.15 Vegetable-first meal sequencing

- **What it does:** eating vegetables before starch/protein slows gastric emptying and blunts glucose excursions; effect on *intake* is smaller than the effect on glucose.
- **Evidence:** A randomised study in young women found vegetable-first sequencing suppressed postprandial glycaemic excursions and variability ([Choi 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42608327/)); the intake effect is supported indirectly through soup/vegetable preloads ([Flood & Rolls 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574705/)).
- **Practical action:** salad or soup first, always. Low cost, no downside.
- **Clinically meaningful?** Clearer for glucose control (relevant if diabetic/prediabetic) than for fat loss.
- **Cost:** Free.

## 4. Ranking: what actually reduces hunger

Ordered by (evidence quality × effect size × affordability), not by how interesting the mechanism is.

| Rank | Lever | Expected effect on hunger/intake | Evidence grade | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Protein at 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day, spread over meals** | Large; spontaneous intake reductions of ~400 kcal/day in a controlled study | **Strong** (multiple RCTs + meta-analyses) | Cheap |
| 2 | **Protein + fibre at every meal** | Large | Strong | Cheap |
| 3 | **Sleep 7–9 h, consistent timing** | Large (~270 kcal/day in sleep-extension RCT) | Strong | Free |
| 4 | **High-volume, low-energy-density meals (400 g veg)** | Large | Strong | Cheap |
| 5 | **Removing hyper-palatable, ultra-processed foods from the house** | Large (~500 kcal/day in a ward study) | Strong | Free (saves money) |
| 6 | **Soup/broth or water preload before the main meal** | Moderate | Moderate (RCTs, small samples) | Free |
| 7 | **Slower eating rate** | Moderate | Moderate–strong (2-week RCT) | Free |
| 8 | **Fixed meal timing (fewer, larger meals vs constant snacking)** | Moderate; individual | Moderate | Free |
| 9 | **Fibre 25–35 g/day including viscous sources** | Moderate | Moderate | Cheap |
| 10 | **Not drinking calorie-containing liquids** | Moderate | Strong for the mechanism | Free |
| 11 | **Vegetable-first sequencing** | Small for intake; good for glucose | Moderate | Free |
| 12 | **Caffeine (coffee/tea, no sugar)** | Small, transient appetite suppression | Weak–moderate | Cheap |
| 13 | **Psyllium 5 g before meals** | Small for hunger; real for constipation/LDL | Moderate for bowel/LDL, weak for weight | Cheap |
| 14 | **Sugar-free gum / sparkling water / black coffee during fasting windows** | Small, mostly behavioural | Weak | Cheap |
| 15 | **"Appetite suppressant" supplements (garcinia, hoodia, raspberry ketone, etc.)** | Not established | Absent/negative | Do not buy |
| 16 | **Extreme fasting to "train" hunger away** | Increases binge risk; no evidence of benefit over moderate IF | Weak | Free but risky |

## 5. The daily hunger-control protocol

Not a calorie prescription — a *structure*. Amounts are scaled by you.

### 5.1 Meal composition rule (applies to every main meal)
1. **400 g non-starchy vegetables** (or 300 g veg + a bowl of soup), always first.
2. **A palm-sized portion of protein** ≈ 25–40 g (150 g chicken/fish, 3 eggs, 200 g lentils + yogurt, 150 g goat).
3. **One starch**, ~150–250 g cooked, chosen for volume: potato, oats-as-porridge, lentils, rice (last choice).
4. **Fat measured, not poured** — 1 tsp–1 tbsp oil maximum, or fat already in the meat/eggs.
5. **Water or unsweetened tea**, not juice or soft drinks.

### 5.2 Protein distribution
- **Total:** roughly 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day for most people losing fat; use **goal weight or lean mass, not total bodyweight**, if you carry a lot of excess fat (explained in `06` §4).
- **Per meal:** 25–40 g, in **2–4 meals**. Distribution beyond 3–4 meals has not been shown to matter much in humans ([Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/); [Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)); older adults need more per dose to get the same response ([Moore 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)).
- **Breakfast is usually the weak point.** Eggs, yogurt, or last night's leftovers beat bread-and-tea.

### 5.3 Fibre targets
- 25–35 g/day total; get ~10 g at each main meal.
- Highest-yield additions here: **lentils/beans, okra, cabbage, oats, guava**.
- Increase by ~5 g/week, and increase water as you do — otherwise you get gas and constipation (`07` §4).

### 5.4 Water
- 400–500 ml water 15–30 min before the main meal.
- Drink to thirst otherwise; **urine pale straw** is the practical target.
- **Do not** force litres of water beyond thirst. Over-drinking causes hyponatraemia in endurance and extreme settings ([Hew-Butler 2015 consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26227507/)) — see `07` §5.

### 5.5 Timing
- **2–3 meals, no grazing** is the easiest structure to control and matches how most people here already eat.
- If you fast (`03`), place the largest meal so it ends 2–3 h before bed; that protects sleep, which protects hunger tomorrow.
- Breakfast: eat it if skipping it makes you eat more later; skip it if it does not. Both work — total intake decides.

### 5.6 Eating speed
Aim for **20+ minutes per main meal**. Practical tricks: no phone, eat with others, water glass before, cut food smaller, chew each mouthful ~15–20 times, stop at 80% full and wait 10 minutes.

### 5.7 Preparation rule ("cook once, eat three times")
Hunger wins when the healthy option requires 40 minutes of work. Cook one large protein and one large vegetable base on two days of the week; assemble meals from them. This single habit outperforms every supplement in `09`.

### 5.8 Snacks
- **Best:** boiled eggs, leftovers, plain yogurt with cucumber, fruit + a few nuts, roasted chickpeas, air-popped popcorn (portioned), soup.
- **Worst:** biscuits, "protein" bars, dates+nut+oil balls, crisps, sweetened juice, sweetened coffee.
- **Rule:** a snack should contain protein or fibre. If it contains neither, it is a dessert.

### 5.9 Between-meal hunger
1. Drink water; wait 15 minutes.
2. If still hungry: **protein or vegetable**, never starch alone.
3. If hungry *every* day at the same hour, that is a schedule problem — move or enlarge the preceding meal rather than adding snacks.

### 5.10 Evening hunger
- Common cause: **under-eating protein at lunch** or an unstructured evening.
- Fix order: (1) bigger protein at lunch; (2) save 300–400 kcal for a large evening meal; (3) brush teeth / stop the kitchen; (4) no screens with food; (5) if genuinely hungry, allow a planned 150 kcal protein snack rather than a "failed day."

### 5.11 Cravings
Cravings are specific and cue-driven, not the same as hunger. Use: remove the cue (do not stock it), delay 10 minutes, substitute (tea, sparkling water, fruit), and **plan them in** — a scheduled small portion weekly is more sustainable than white-knuckling it and bingeing.

## 6. Hunger in a deficit: what is normal and what is not

| Signal | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Hunger before meals, satisfied by a normal meal | **Normal** | Continue |
| Hunger that fades in 15–20 min ("hunger wave") | Normal; ghrelin is pulsatile | Drink water, wait |
| Constant, gnawing hunger with low mood, poor sleep, cold intolerance, hair loss, no menstrual cycles | **Excessive restriction** | Increase intake, stop the deficit, see a doctor if persistent |
| Dizziness, palpitations, confusion, fainting | **Not normal** — see `03` §7 | Eat; seek care if recurrent |
| Binge urges, secrecy, guilt, purging | **Disordered eating pattern** | Stop dieting; seek professional help (see `04` §6) |

**The rule this project will never break:** dangerous symptoms are never to be suppressed, "pushed through," or re-interpreted as weakness.

## 7. Summary

### 7.1 Strongest verified findings
1. Protein is the most satiating macronutrient and raises spontaneous fullness: +441 kcal/day spontaneous intake reduction at ~30% protein ([Weigle 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/)); ghrelin suppression ([Yang 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23824257/)).
2. Energy density drives intake: ultra-processed vs unprocessed diets differed by **+508 kcal/day** with matched macros ([Hall 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/)); low-energy-density diets improved maintenance ([Karimi 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27904578/)).
3. Sleep extension cuts intake by ~270 kcal/day ([Tasali 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35129580/)); short sleep shifts loss away from fat and toward muscle ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)).
4. Eating rate has sustained effects on intake ([Forde 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41314613/)).
5. Liquids are less satiating than solids ([Cassady 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22258267/)); pulses improve appetite control ([John 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37395146/)).
6. Appetite hormones stay altered after weight loss ([Sumithran 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22029981/)) — hunger in maintenance is physiological, not a character flaw.

### 7.2 Most practical actions
Protein at every meal; 400 g vegetables; soup first; sleep 7–9 h; slower eating; no calorie liquids; no trigger foods in the house; fixed meal times.

### 7.3 Cheapest effective options
Eggs, chicken, goat, lentils, cabbage, potato, okra, oats, water, sleep. **Zero-purchase hunger control is genuinely available.**

### 7.4 What is unnecessary
Appetite-suppressant supplements, "GLP-1 booster" products, glucomannan unless you enjoy it (small effect, [−0.79 kg in one meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18842808/)), slimming teas, detox drinks, apple cider vinegar, small frequent meals as a rule (no advantage over fewer larger meals), and expensive protein powders if you eat eggs and chicken.

### 7.5 What is unsupported or misleading
- "Certain foods raise GLP-1 like Ozempic" — mechanism only, no clinical equivalence.
- "Insulin blocks fat burning / you must lower insulin to lose fat" — not supported by controlled human comparisons.
- "You can train yourself to stop feeling hunger" — appetite hormones adapt to oppose loss ([Sumithran 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22029981/)); you manage hunger, you do not eliminate it.
- "Ghrelin blockers" as consumer products — none is established.
- Any specific percent claim for how much a food "boosts metabolism."

### 7.6 Important risks
Chronic under-eating with persistent severe hunger, hair loss, cold intolerance or loss of libido = stop and reassess; fibre loading without water = constipation/bloating; excessive water or salt loading = electrolyte risk; treating binge urges with more restriction = worsening cycle.

### 7.7 Concise action plan
1. Pick **2 protein anchors** you can cook repeatedly (eggs + chicken/fish, or chicken + lentils).
2. Fill half of every plate with vegetables, and eat them first.
3. Set a fixed **sleep window** before you touch your diet.
4. Remove one hyper-palatable food from your home this week — permanently.
5. Eat one meal a day with **no screen and no rush**, aiming for 20 minutes.
6. Track only: protein at each meal, vegetable volume, sleep hours. Nothing else.
