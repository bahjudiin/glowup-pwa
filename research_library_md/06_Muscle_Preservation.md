# 06 — Muscle Preservation During Fat Loss

*Phase 2 module, file 6 of 12. Protein's appetite effects are in `02` §3.3; the deficit/plateau logic in `08`.*

---

## 1. Purpose

Every kilogram you lose in a deficit is a mix of fat, water and lean tissue. **The goal of this file is to make that mix as favourable as possible with the least equipment, money and time** — because losing 8 kg where 3 kg is muscle is a worse outcome than losing 6 kg of fat.

This file does **not** repeat Phase-1 training programming (`03_BODY_RECOMPOSITION_AND_MUSCLE_RETENTION.md`, `10_EXERCISE_AND_NEAT.md`) or the testosterone/longevity and supplement material (`12_MENS_HEALTH_AND_LONGEVITY.md`, `16_SUPPLEMENTS_EVIDENCE_REVIEW.md`).

## 2. What actually protects muscle — ranked

| Rank | Lever | Effect | Evidence | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Resistance training** (2–3 sessions/week, progressive) | Large — the dominant signal to retain muscle in a deficit | Strong | Free |
| 2 | **Adequate protein** (~1.2–1.6 g/kg/day of an appropriate reference weight, §4) | Large | Strong | Cheap |
| 3 | **Moderate, not extreme, deficit** | Large — bigger deficits accelerate lean loss | Strong | Free |
| 4 | **Enough sleep (7–9 h)** | Moderate–large: short sleep increased fat-free-mass loss by ~60% at matched calories ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)) | Strong | Free |
| 5 | **Protein spread over 2–4 meals, 25–40 g each** | Moderate | Moderate–strong ([Schoenfeld 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/); [Moore 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)) | Cheap |
| 6 | **Avoid very long fasting windows (< 6 h eating window)** | Moderate — TREAT found lean-mass loss with 16:8 ([TREAT 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32986097/)) | Moderate | Free |
| 7 | **Daily walking / non-exercise activity** | Small–moderate (energy balance and recovery) | Moderate | Free |
| 8 | **Creatine monohydrate** | Small — supports training performance and lean mass, not a fat-loss agent | Moderate | Cheap (`09`) |
| 9 | **Leucine/HMB/BCAA supplements** | Small–none over adequate total protein | Weak | Not worth it |
| 10 | **Protein timing around training** | Small once daily total is met | Weak–moderate | Free |

## 3. Protein: how much, and why the usual numbers mislead

### 3.1 The core evidence
- Meta-analysis of protein supplementation combined with resistance training: additional protein increases strength and lean mass gain, with benefits plateauing at high intakes; the pooled regression suggested **~1.62 g/kg/day** as the intake above which added protein gave little further benefit ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)).
- In a deficit specifically, resistance-trained lean athletes benefit from higher intakes — a systematic review of protein during caloric restriction in that population concluded that higher protein (~2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass) supported lean-mass retention ([Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/)). **Note the denominator: fat-free mass, not total bodyweight.**
- Per-meal distribution: muscle protein synthesis responds dose-dependently up to ~0.4 g/kg per meal, with diminishing returns per meal and no clear benefit to more than ~4 protein feedings ([Schoenfeld & Aragon 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/)); even distribution outperformed skewed distribution in a controlled trial ([Areta 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/)).
- Older adults require **more protein per dose** to achieve the same anabolic response ([Moore 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)).

### 3.2 The trap: "1.6 g/kg of bodyweight" applied to everyone
This is the most common error in fat-loss advice, and it matters.

A man at 130 kg with 45% body fat has ~71 kg of fat-free mass. Applying 1.6 g/kg of **total** bodyweight gives **208 g protein/day** — an enormous, expensive intake that provides no extra muscle protection and crowds out vegetables, fibre and the calorie deficit itself. Applying it to an appropriate reference weight gives **110–125 g/day**, which is plenty.

### 3.3 Three ways to set the target, in order of preference

| Method | Formula | Example: 130 kg man, ~40% fat (≈78 kg lean) | Example: 85 kg man, ~18% fat (≈70 kg lean) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Reference/goal weight** *(recommended default)* | 1.2–1.6 g × kg of a realistic goal weight or "weight at BMI 25" | Goal 90 kg → **110–145 g/day** | Goal 78 kg → **95–125 g/day** |
| **2. Lean mass (fat-free mass)** | 1.6–2.2 g × kg FFM if you know it from DEXA/BIA | 1.6–2.0 × 78 → **125–155 g/day** | 1.8 × 70 → **126 g/day** |
| **3. Total bodyweight** *(only if not carrying large excess fat)* | 1.2–1.6 g × kg total | Would give 155–208 g — **too high, do not use** | 102–136 g/day — reasonable |

**Practical rule of thumb if you have no measurements:** aim for **1.6–1.8 g/kg of your *goal* weight**, or **~110–140 g/day for most adult men**, whichever is higher when lean and whichever is *lower* when carrying a lot of excess fat. Then treat protein as "achieved" once you're within that band — more is not better.

### 3.4 How to hit it cheaply here (food, not powder)

| Food | Portion | Protein |
|---|---|---|
| Eggs | 3 whole | ~19 g |
| Chicken breast/thigh | 150 g cooked | ~37–46 g |
| Goat | 150 g cooked | ~40 g |
| Local fish | 150 g cooked | ~30–39 g |
| Tuna, canned in water | 1 can (~140 g drained) | ~30 g |
| Lentils, cooked | 300 g | ~27 g |
| Beans/chickpeas, cooked | 300 g | ~26 g |
| Plain yogurt | 250 g | ~9 g (weak — see `01` §5) |
| Greek yogurt | 200 g | ~20 g |
| Milk | 500 ml | ~16 g |

A day of 3 eggs + 150 g chicken + 150 g fish + 200 g lentils ≈ **115 g protein** — no supplements needed. **Whey is a convenience, not a requirement** (`09` §5).

## 4. Resistance training: the minimum effective dose

The point in a deficit is **retention**, not maximal growth. That changes the requirement substantially: you need enough tension and enough progression to tell the body "keep this tissue," not an optimal hypertrophy programme.

**Minimum effective plan (no equipment, at home, 25–30 min, 2–3×/week):**

| Session | Exercises | Sets × reps |
|---|---|---|
| A | Squat, push-up (or knee push-up), split squat, plank | 3 × 8–15 each |
| B | Glute bridge / single-leg bridge, pike push-up or chair dip, row with a bag/bottle/towel, hollow hold | 3 × 8–15 each |

**How to progress without weights:** slow the lowering phase (3 seconds), increase range of motion, reduce rest, add a set, move to a harder variation (knee → full push-up; squat → split squat → single-leg squat to a chair), or fill a bag with water bottles/sand.

**Two rules that matter more than the programme:**
1. **Progressive overload** — something must get harder every 1–2 weeks.
2. **Do not add hard training to a long fast.** Train fed, or at least not 20+ hours fasted and dehydrated.

**Cardio/walking:** 7,000–10,000 steps/day is a reasonable, unobtrusive target that supports energy balance without adding hunger or recovery cost. Higher step counts are associated with better hormonal/energy profiles in observational data ([Macena 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34526156/)), but this is associational, not causal proof.

## 5. Deficit size, recovery and the three ways people lose muscle

| Cause | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Deficit too large** (e.g. > 1% bodyweight/week sustained) | Strength falls, constant fatigue, cold, poor sleep, rapid scale drop | Slow down; a rate closer to ~0.5–1% bodyweight/week preserves lean mass better ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/) compared slow vs fast loss and found lean mass preserved in the slower group) |
| **Protein too low / meals too few** | Protein < ~1.2 g/kg; OMAD-style eating | Raise protein; spread over 2–4 meals |
| **No resistance training** | Scale drops, waist drops slowly, strength drops, appearance "soft" | Add 2×/week resistance work |

**Sleep and alcohol** belong here too: short sleep shifts loss toward lean tissue ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)), and heavy alcohol intake impairs recovery and training quality.

## 6. Fasting and muscle protein synthesis

- **Muscle protein synthesis (MPS)** falls during fasting and rises with protein feeding; that is not controversial. What matters is the **24-hour total**, not the fasted hours.
- The practical risk is not the fast itself but what it does to **protein intake and training quality**: short windows (< 6 h) make it hard to fit 2–4 protein-sufficient meals, and fasting training reduces performance.
- **Observed outcome:** in TREAT, the 16:8 group lost lean mass (−0.16 kg/m², P=0.005) while total weight change was null ([TREAT 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32986097/)).
- **Mitigation:** eating window ≥ 8 h; 2–3 protein feedings of 30–40 g inside it; resistance training 2–3×/week; do not train fasted for hard sessions.
- **Do not** believe "fasting is muscle-sparing" — protein feeding is the anabolic signal, and its absence is the cost.

## 7. Age-related muscle loss (and why it changes the plan)

- After ~40, anabolic resistance develops: the same protein dose produces less MPS ([Moore 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)). Older men therefore need **more protein per meal** (~30–40 g) and **resistance training is non-negotiable**.
- Sarcopenia (age-related muscle loss) is worsened by rapid weight loss without protein/training; recent syntheses in older adults support combined protein + resistance training rather than protein alone ([Yang 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42712362/); [Hernandez-Martinez 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42700006/)).
- **Implication:** if you are over ~50, prioritise a slower deficit and higher per-meal protein over speed of loss.

**Also relevant:** weight loss achieved with GLP-1 receptor agonists includes lean mass, and preserving it requires the same levers — protein, resistance training, adequate deficit ([Sukurica 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42839755/); [Wilkinson 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42806732/)). The drug does not remove the need for training.

## 8. How to tell whether you are losing fat, water, or muscle

No bathroom method measures this directly. But the combination below is informative:

| Signal | Fat loss | Water loss | Muscle loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scale | Down steadily, 0.3–1 kg/week | Fast drop (1.5–3 kg in days), often after a high-carb/salty day reversal | Down, often faster than expected |
| Waist (at navel) | Down ~1 cm per 1–1.5 kg fat lost | Fluctuates ±1–2 cm day to day | Down slowly relative to weight |
| Strength / reps | Maintained or up | Unchanged | **Falls** — the clearest signal |
| Appearance | Softer but smaller; clothes looser | Puffy, then "deflated" | Flatter but "stringy"; arms/legs thinner |
| Measurements | Arms/chest stable, waist down | Everything slightly down then back | Arms/chest/legs all down |
| Energy/mood | Stable | Transient | Fatigue, poor recovery, cold |
| Grip/performance | Maintained | — | Declining |

**Practical measurement routine (60 seconds/week):**
1. Weigh daily (or 3×/week) and use the **7-day average**, never a single day.
2. Waist at navel, same time of day, once a week.
3. One strength benchmark: max push-ups, or reps at a fixed load, every 2 weeks.
4. Optional: monthly photo, same light and pose.

**Red flags that you are losing muscle:** strength down two sessions in a row; persistent fatigue; hair loss and feeling cold; libido collapse; sleep deterioration. Response: **eat more, train less volume, raise protein** — not "push harder."

**Scale-based cautions**
- A **1–2 kg jump overnight** is water/glycogen/food weight, not fat. Fat cannot be gained that fast.
- A **plateau of 10–14 days** with a stable waist is usually water and adaptation, not "starvation mode" (`08`).
- BIA scales (body-fat scales) are **inaccurate in absolute terms** and affected by hydration; use the *trend* only, if at all.

## 9. Summary

### 9.1 Strongest verified findings
1. **Resistance training + higher protein preserves lean mass** during energy restriction; combining them outperforms calorie restriction alone ([Garthe 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/); [Helms 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/)).
2. **~1.6 g/kg is the point of diminishing returns** for protein's effect on training gains ([Morton 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/)) — and that should be applied to a sensible reference weight, not to total bodyweight in obesity.
3. **Per-meal doses of ~0.4 g/kg, spread over 2–4 meals, are optimal** ([Schoenfeld 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29497353/)); older adults need more per dose ([Moore 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25056502/)).
4. **Short sleep increases lean-mass loss by ~60%** at matched calories ([Nedeltcheva 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20921542/)).
5. **16:8 without protein/training care cost lean mass** in a 12-week RCT ([TREAT 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32986097/)).
6. **GLP-1-induced weight loss also includes lean mass**, and the preserving levers are unchanged ([Sukurica 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42839755/)).

### 9.2 Most practical actions
Protein 110–140 g/day from food; 2–3 home resistance sessions/week with progression; keep the deficit moderate; sleep 7–9 h; walk 7–10k steps; measure strength, not just weight.

### 9.3 Cheapest effective options
Eggs, chicken, goat, local fish, lentils, and a floor. This is a genuinely free programme. Creatine (~$0.16–0.21/day, `09`) is the only supplement with even a modest case.

### 9.4 What is unnecessary
BCAAs, HMB, glutamine, testosterone "boosters," mass gainers, pre-workouts, gym memberships (for the first 6 months), and protein powder if you can eat 3 eggs + 300 g of animal protein a day.

### 9.5 What is unsupported or misleading
- "You must eat 2.2 g/kg bodyweight to keep muscle" — not supported, and actively counterproductive in obesity.
- "Fasting preserves muscle better" — contradicted by the lean-mass data.
- "Cardio burns muscle" — false at normal volumes; a large deficit plus no resistance training does.
- "You can't build muscle in a deficit" — you can, especially as a beginner; retention is the priority but gain is possible.
- Spot reduction, "muscle confusion," and any claim that a supplement prevents muscle loss without training.

### 9.6 Important risks
Deficits faster than ~1%/week; training through pain (tendon/joint injury); protein intakes so high they displace vegetables and fibre; kidney caution in pre-existing kidney disease; eating-disorder risk when protein rules become rigid.

### 9.7 Concise action plan
1. Calculate a protein target from **goal weight or lean mass** (§3.3) and write it on the fridge.
2. Hit it in **3 meals** — eggs at breakfast, meat/fish at lunch, lentils/yogurt at dinner.
3. Do **Session A** tonight: squats, push-ups, split squats, plank — 3 sets each.
4. Add **one rep or one second** to each exercise every week.
5. Weigh daily, judge weekly; measure waist once a week; test push-ups every two weeks.
6. If strength falls for two consecutive tests: eat 200–300 kcal more, keep training.
