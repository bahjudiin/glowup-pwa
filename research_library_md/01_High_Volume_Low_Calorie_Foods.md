# 01 — High-Volume, Low-Calorie Foods (Volume Eating)

*Phase 2 module, file 1 of 12. Companion files: `02_Hunger_Control_and_Satiety.md` (mechanisms), `07_Hydration_Electrolytes_and_Digestion.md` (fibre tolerance), `09_Amazon_iHerb_Products.md` (what to buy).*

---

## 1. Purpose and scope

This file answers one practical question: **which foods let you eat the largest physical amount of food — and stay full the longest — for the fewest calories, that you can actually buy in Mogadishu?**

It does **not** repeat Phase-1 material on calorie targets, macronutrient frameworks or obesity pharmacology (see `00_MASTER_SUMMARY.md`, files `01`–`21`). It is a food-selection file: tables, rankings, costs, and the specific evidence for the satiety claim attached to each food.

---

## 2. How every number in this file was produced (read this first)

**Nutrition data.** All calorie, protein, fibre, water and potassium values are per **100 g, as-sold/raw unless stated**, taken from the **USDA FoodData Central "SR Legacy"** database (the complete dataset was downloaded and queried offline — see `11_Evidence_and_References.md` §2.3).

*Link format note:* USDA FoodData Central does not expose a stable per-record URL that could be verified for every food here, so each food name links to a **FoodData Central search for that record's FDC ID** (e.g. `https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-search?query=169975`). Paste the FDC ID shown in the link into the search box to open the record. This is a clearly-labelled search link, not a guaranteed deep link. SR Legacy is a reference database of *average* composition; a Somali cabbage or goat carcass will not match it to the decimal. Treat values as ±10–15%.

**Price labels.** Every price carries exactly one of three labels:

| Label | Meaning in this project |
|---|---|
| **Verified local price** | A price the researcher personally confirmed in a Mogadishu shop/market on a stated date. |
| **Estimated price** | A price from a third-party reporting source (crowdsourced cost-of-living sites or a Somali market-price dashboard), used as an order-of-magnitude guide only. |
| **Unknown** | No source found. Not guessed. |

> **Honest disclosure:** after an explicit search, **no price in this file qualifies as "Verified local price."** Nothing was checked at a stall in person. Prices marked **Estimated** come from three third-party sources, and they disagree with each other by up to 2× on the same item — which is exactly why they cannot be treated as verified:
> - Somali Researchers Association market dashboard, Mogadishu column — <https://www.sra.so/market-price/>
> - Numbeo, Mogadishu food prices — <https://www.numbeo.com/food-prices/in/Mogadishu-Somalia>
> - Livingcost.org, Mogadishu — <https://livingcost.org/cost/somalia/mogadishu>
>
> Use the numbers to rank foods against each other, not to budget to the dollar. **Any item marked Unknown may be cheap and available — imported packaged goods (whey, psyllium, canned tuna) in particular are usually *more* expensive locally than the international retail prices quoted in file `09`.**

**Satiety evidence column.** Three grades:
- **Direct RCT** — a randomised trial measured intake or satiety for *that food* (or that food class) in humans.
- **Indirect** — the food scores well on a validated satiety index, or belongs to a food class with RCT support (e.g. "vegetable-first", "high water content"), but was not itself tested.
- **None** — plausible but untested. Stated plainly.

A mechanistic reason is never treated as proof of a real-world outcome.

### 7.5 Best fast-breaking foods (for `03`/`04`)

Ranked for breaking a 16–24 h fast: filling, gentle, protein-first, not a binge trigger.

1. **Broth or soup** (warm, salty, 6 kcal/100 g) — 10 minutes before solid food.
2. **Eggs** — protein-dense, self-limiting, minimal volume shock.
3. **Chicken or fish with vegetables** — protein + fibre + water in one plate.
4. **Lentils/beans** — protein + fibre, but higher FODMAP load; skip if you get gas early in fasting adaptation.
5. **Potato** — if you tolerate it; highest satiety per calorie of any starch.
6. **Fruit (papaya/orange)** — fine, but do not make it the whole meal; it does not hold hunger.
7. **Avoid as the first meal:** large volumes of oil/fried food, very spicy food, energy drinks, coffee on an empty stomach if you are prone to reflux, and anything you specifically crave in large amounts (see `04` §6 on binge-restrict cycles).

## 8. Do potato, oats and legumes actually help fat loss through satiety?

This deserves a direct answer, because these three are the most contested items in the "volume eating" world.

**What is well supported**
- **Potatoes:** boiled/baked potato produced the **highest satiety index score of any food** in Holt's 1995 comparison of 38 foods tested at equal 240 kcal portions ([Holt 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7498104/)). Higher satiety ratings in a lab are *not* the same as weight loss, but they are a legitimate proxy, and potatoes are cheap and available here at ~93 kcal/100 g with 535 mg potassium.
- **Oats:** porridge oats scored high in the same index; oat β-glucan is viscous and slows gastric emptying, and higher-fibre/higher-viscosity carbohydrate sources reduce appetite vs refined sources. **Caveat:** dry oats are 379 kcal/100 g — the satiety benefit depends entirely on cooking them with water into a large volume. Oats with milk, honey and dried fruit is a 500 kcal breakfast that fills like a 250 kcal one.
- **Legumes:** a review of dietary pulses reports improved appetite control and intake in overweight/obese adults ([John 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37395146/)), and lentils/beans give 6–8 g fibre per 100 g with 7–10 g protein. This is one of the best-evidenced food-class interventions in this entire module.

**What is not supported**
- That any of these foods "boosts metabolism," "burns fat," or has a special fat-loss property beyond **being filling per calorie and displacing more calorie-dense food**. No food has that property.
- That the glycemic index of potato matters enough to avoid it. In a whole-diet context where calories and protein are matched, potato vs another starch does not determine fat loss.
- That legumes must be avoided because of "anti-nutrients." Cooking destroys most lectin activity; phytates have modest mineral-binding effects that matter mainly in deficient diets.

**Practical verdict:** if you are in a deficit and hungry, **potatoes, oats-as-porridge, and lentils/beans are the three starches that buy you the most fullness per calorie**, and they are cheap. That is a satiety argument, not a metabolic one. If you personally find oats trigger overeating (sweet toppings), use potato instead.

## 9. Where plant-based "GLP-1" claims go wrong

You will encounter claims that high-fibre or high-protein foods "raise GLP-1 like Ozempic." The honest position:

- Gut hormones including GLP-1, PYY and CCK *are* released in response to nutrients, and fibre/protein meals *do* raise them measurably (see `02` §7, and [Brennan 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22556143/) for fat/protein/carbohydrate effects on CCK and PYY).
- The physiological post-meal rise from food is **orders of magnitude smaller and shorter** than the sustained pharmacological levels produced by GLP-1 receptor agonists.
- **No food or food pattern has been shown to produce clinical weight loss equivalent to a GLP-1 drug.** Nothing in this module claims otherwise. Foods can help you *feel full and eat less*; they are not drug substitutes.

## 10. Risks, caveats and things this file deliberately does not say

| Risk | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Bloating/gas** | A sudden jump to 30–40 g fibre/day from a low baseline causes gas, bloating and loose stools. Increase by ~5 g/week and increase fluid with it (see `07`). |
| **Very high raw-vegetable volumes** | Large raw cabbage/broccoli loads can worsen reflux and IBS-type symptoms. Cooking is fine. |
| **Goitrogens** | Cooking largely inactivates the relevant enzymes in cabbage/cauliflower; at normal intakes with adequate iodine this is not a practical concern. Not a reason to limit vegetables. |
| **Potassium** | Potato (535 mg), spinach (558), tomato paste (1014), white beans (561) are high-potassium foods. If you have **kidney disease** or take ACE inhibitors/spironolactone, discuss with your doctor before deliberately loading these. |
| **Food safety** | Raw salads washed in unsafe water are a real risk in many settings — wash with safe water, or use cooked vegetables. |
| **Disordered eating** | "Volume eating" can tip into using huge low-calorie volumes to avoid eating. If food is becoming a system of rules rather than meals, stop and read `04` §6 and `08` §9. |
| **Not said on purpose** | No food on this list is a "negative-calorie food," no food "melts fat," and no combination "resets your metabolism." |

## 11. Summary

### 11.1 Strongest verified findings
1. **Soup/water-rich form beats dry form.** Soup preloads reduced meal energy intake across forms ([Flood & Rolls 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574705/)); liquid calories are less satiating than solid ([Cassady 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22258267/)).
2. **Boiled/baked potato topped the satiety index** of 38 foods tested at equal calories ([Holt 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7498104/)) — a real, replicated-in-spirit finding, and potatoes are cheap here.
3. **Dietary pulses improve appetite control** in overweight/obese adults ([John 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37395146/)).
4. **Fibre intake in the 25–29 g/day range is associated with the best health outcomes**, with benefit continuing above 30 g/day ([Reynolds 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30638909/)).
5. **Ultra-processed, hyper-palatable, high-energy-density food drives passive overeating** (+508 kcal/day in a controlled ward study, [Hall 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/)); the mechanism is energy density and eating rate, not willpower.

### 11.2 Most practical actions
1. Build every lunch and dinner around **400 g of non-starchy vegetable + 120–150 g protein**, then add starch.
2. Make **cabbage, tomato, eggplant, onion, carrot** your default vegetable set — cheapest and always available.
3. Start the largest meal with **soup or broth**, wait 10 minutes, then eat.
4. Use **potato, oats-as-porridge, lentils/beans** as your starches, not bread/pasta/rice, when hunger is the problem.
5. Choose **papaya, watermelon, orange, banana, guava** as fruit; skip imported apples/berries.
6. Keep **eggs** as the default cheap protein; whole chicken/thigh over breast for cost.

### 11.3 Cheapest effective options
Cabbage; eggs; potato; tomato-onion-eggplant stew; homemade broth; bananas/papaya; dry lentils/mung beans if stocked; seasonal watermelon. These alone can implement the entire volume strategy.

### 11.4 What is unnecessary
Whey protein (unless you cannot hit protein from food), "zero-calorie" shirataki noodles, imported berries, sugar-free jelly, cauliflower rice substitutes when actual cauliflower is unavailable, apple cider vinegar, "detox" teas, and any product marketed as a "negative-calorie" or "fat-burning" food.

### 11.5 What is unsupported or misleading
- "Volume eating means unlimited calories" — no; a 1,200 kcal deficit still requires a deficit.
- "High-fibre foods raise GLP-1 like medication" — mechanism only; no clinical equivalence.
- Precise claims that a specific food "increases metabolism by X%".
- Any food-level claim that survives only as a mechanistic story.

### 11.6 Important risks
Fibre increases too fast → gas/bloating; raw-salad food safety; high-potassium foods with kidney disease or ACE inhibitors/spironolactone; oil used to make vegetables "tasty" silently adding 100–300 kcal; volume eating as a cover for restrictive eating.
